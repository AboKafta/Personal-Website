01Highlights

  • Two-layer heart-shaped board driving 14 LEDs from a 555 timer, with a potentiometer for blink speed; validated in TinkerCAD, then captured, sourced and routed in KiCad.
  • Moved from a 9 V battery to a single 3 V coin cell with a low-voltage CMOS 555 and 2 V LEDs, balancing coin-cell life against brightness.

03Initial prototype (summer 2025)

I first made a simple prototype in the summer of 2025 as a gift for my girlfriend. After some tutorials and many hours, I had a design, though I stumbled on things like placing footprints, design errors, and generating a BOM. This is how it was supposed to look after ordering through JLCPCB, but once I saw the cost, and wasn’t sure it would even work, I didn’t manufacture it. A lot of steps were missed (the correct factory specifications, the wrong Gerber file, and so on), and it mixed through-hole and SMD parts.

04Second iteration (2026)

The goal was to make roughly the same board, but this time use a 555 timer to make it blink, use only through-hole components so it’s easy to solder, and make it smaller and nicer to look at.

Prototype

Using a guide from Instructables, I built a prototype in TinkerCAD to check that everything worked before making a schematic. My modifications:

  • A potentiometer acting as a rheostat, so the blink speed can be changed instead of being fixed.
  • 13 additional LEDs, each with a 100 Ω resistor.
  • A 3 V coin cell instead of a 9 V battery for convenience, with a switch added.

Schematic

A helpful KiCad tutorial. In KiCad, the base schematic was transformed from a single-LED 555 blinker into the full heart circuit:

Notes:

  • Higher LED resistor values extend battery life, but the LEDs won’t be as bright.
  • If you swap LED colors, keep the forward voltage around 2 V. Anything above 3 V means dim lights and short battery life.
  • The coin cell came from Amazon rather than DigiKey.
  • The 50 Ω LED resistors keep the current at a safe level instead of relying on the coin cell’s internal resistance, which is prone to spikes.
  • Make sure the 555 timer you choose runs at 3 V; some need 4.5 V or more.

Parts

Everything came from DigiKey with symbols and footprints, except the yellow LEDs, which use a basic 5 mm LED footprint from KiCad.

  • Switch
  • 555 timer (ICM7555)
  • 1k Ω resistor
  • 1 µF capacitor
  • Yellow LEDs
  • 50 Ω resistors
  • 1 MΩ potentiometer

After that, run DRC, and only continue if there are no errors. Set the limits of your chosen PCB manufacturer before starting the layout.

05PCB layout

To get the heart shape, I made two semicircles and extended two lines from their endpoints until they intersected. Then I updated the PCB from the schematic and laid out the components, placing each resistor next to its LED so no vias were needed. After refilling and rechecking DRC with zero warnings or errors, I added finishing touches on the silkscreen.

Total cost: $46 ($13 for the PCB, $33 for parts).

06Soldering

The LEDs worked while I was soldering, then stopped. I first thought they were drawing too much current and removed some, but it still didn’t work. Measuring current with my multimeter, the lights came on when I touched the leads, except for two, whose legs were touching each other. After fixing that, every light worked. I reordered LEDs, but ended up burning a pad off one of them.
