01Highlights

  • Designed and brought up a four-layer board following Raspberry Pi’s design guide: 12 MHz crystal, QSPI boot flash, on-chip switching regulator, 3.3 V LDO, and USB-C with ESD protection.
  • Moved from two to four layers for a clean MCU via escape and an unbroken ground plane under the USB pair and crystal, trading fab cost for signal integrity.
  • Re-sourced the BOM to in-stock basic passives after the first quote came back inflated by minimum-order reels and special-part fees, cutting assembly cost by 50%.

02Revision history

  1. Schematic

    Every section of the hardware design guide captured on one A2 sheet, with ERC after each section.

  2. Layout, first try

    DigiKey footprints threw errors, so the layout was restarted with LCSC parts and a JLCPCB rule set.

  3. Four-layer layout

    Ground on In1, 3V3 and 1V1 pours on In2, ground on the back; routing on the outer layers.

  4. Revision

    Regulator capacitors resized so a trace fits between them, as the datasheet shows.

  5. Bring-up

    5 V, 3.3 V and 1.1 V rails checked; display, buttons, LED and every GPIO tested.

03System architecture

04Goals

The main goal of this project was to learn the more in-depth parts of PCB design, such as power regulation, decoupling capacitors, crystal oscillators, and USB routing, by designing a custom board around the RP2350 chip. I added an LCD display, an LED, and some buttons to personalize the board. I did everything from the ground up rather than downloading the example schematic, which I kept only as a reference to double-check my work.

I chose the RP2350 for its good documentation and low cost, and because I’d like to code more in MicroPython down the road. I had planned on the RP2040 at first, but chose the RP2350 for its faster computation. I went with the 30-GPIO model since I don’t need the extra 18 GPIOs the 48-GPIO model offers, and used JLCPCB for all component ordering.

05Schematic

I kept everything on one schematic instead of a hierarchical system to keep it simple and readable on one page, and changed the sheet size from A4 to A2. Symbols and footprints for the RP2350A came from the reference files, which I added to KiCad as libraries. The rest came from the KiCad library or were imported when necessary. I assigned footprints section by section and ran ERC after each section, adding power flags whenever ERC required them.

Voltage regulator

I didn’t copy the hardware design guide’s designators, since that felt unnecessary. One thing to note is that a specific inductor part must be used because of its orientation: a random inductor could disrupt the circuit through the electromagnetic field it creates. The capacitors have a voltage rating of about 10 V, use X5R or X7R dielectric, and have under 10% tolerance.

I used labels instead of wires to keep the circuit neat. Three warnings came up about duplicate net names, so I renamed them after the power line they connect to, then used highlight net to confirm everything connected as it should.

Input supply

The hardware design guide uses a Micro-USB connector, which almost no one has these days, so I switched to a 16-pin USB Type-C connector that isn’t power-only. On this part both sides are connected, so there is one VBUS and one GND instead of separate A and B pins.

The wiring is a bit different for Type-C. DN1/DN2 and DP1/DP2 are tied together; think of them as D− and D+ in the example schematic. GND and shield are both tied to ground, and VBUS connects to the LDO just like the example. The new part is CC1 and CC2: Micro-USB keeps VBUS on constantly, while Type-C uses the CC pins and their pull-down resistors to detect a real connection. The pull-downs are 5.1k Ω (reference).

Decoupling capacitors

I followed the datasheet but used labels instead of wires to keep it neat. Keeping each capacitor close to its pin was the key note to remember for layout.

Primary flash

There are two variants of the W25Q128JVS memory chip. I chose the W25Q128JVSIQ because quad mode is enabled automatically, unlike the M version, which requires setup. The Flash_SS label is there for clearer referencing during layout, and USB_BOOT is labeled the same way, with a line above it to mark it as active-low.

The two resistors marked DNF aren’t needed for this flash chip. At first I replaced them with wires, which caused warnings; it took some time to realize I had to delete those sections entirely instead of bridging the gaps. The 0 Ω resistor lets the board be reconfigured later. No specific button is specified for this circuit, so I chose a small SMD push button, and I used a different symbol from the KiCad library, so the wiring looks a bit different from the reference.

Crystal oscillator

I imported the symbol and footprint shown on DigiKey. After wiring, I got unspecified pin type warnings because the symbol’s author hadn’t set the pins’ electrical types, so I set them to passive in the symbol editor and everything went well.

IOs

Two termination resistors sit on the USB data lines and need to be placed close to the chip. I first thought I didn’t need them because I was using a Type-C cable, but they relate to USB signaling, not the connector.

The reference has a whole row of ground pins next to the GPIO pins. Plenty of ground limits impedance and electromagnetic interference, but I wanted to be able to flip the board onto a breadboard if needed. So instead of two 02×18 headers I used single-row headers, with grounds placed periodically to reduce the side effects. There is also an extra decoupling capacitor next to the 3.3 V source.

LCD, buttons, and LED

Because some GPIOs were now needed for these parts, the first IO map was scrapped. The buttons each go from a GPIO to ground, using four GPIOs. The LED is wired as GPIO25 → resistor → LED → GND.

The LCD was more complicated. I chose the Adafruit 3533 RGB display because it’s small, compact, and RGB. It uses 10 GPIO pins instead of the 2 a basic I2C OLED would, but 3 of them are shared: the SPI0 pins can be shared because they’re a bus designed for multiple devices.

The LCD’s symbol is a basic 1×11 connector, and on the board it sits on female headers so the display can be removed. I renamed the pins to match the LCD, got its exact dimensions, and made a custom footprint in the footprint editor so I wouldn’t accidentally place buttons under it. Thanks to HTM Workshop for the guide.

Finishing touches and review

  • Skipped the optional debug connector from the guide.
  • Connected the ground pad under the chip to ground.
  • Added a button directly to the RUN pin and four mounting holes.
  • Added 4.7k Ω series resistors to the buttons instead of relying on the chip’s internal resistors.
  • Dropped the LED resistor to 150 Ω to make it brighter.
  • Added ESD protection on the USB port, since it will be plugged and unplugged constantly.

06Layout

Before anything else, I set the board restrictions to JLCPCB’s rules and decided on a four-layer board to make routing easier. My first attempt used DigiKey symbols and footprints and gave me a lot of errors, so I restarted the layout file, switched to LCSC components, and built the BOM first. Along the way, the 1×17 headers became 1×19 (1×17 wasn’t available), the 0 Ω resistor was removed, the 27 Ω resistors became 22 Ω, and the LED resistor went from 150 Ω to 120 Ω.

Placement

I stuck to the hardware guide’s recommendations with the minimal design file open next to me. Capacitors stay close to their pins, decoupling capacitors sit by their 1V1 or 3V3 pins, and the LDO and converter are close to the USB-C connector. My first placement put everything so close to the chip that there was no room to route, so I spread components out.

Routing

I used wider traces for the USB routes (0.6 mm) and 0.3 mm traces for power wherever I could, and mostly followed the reference design’s wiring. The 1V1 and 3V3 lines had to cross, so I used vias to route them around each other. The IOs were scrambled at first, so I went back to the schematic and reassigned the button GPIOs so they routed more cleanly.

The final board has four layers: a ground pour on In1, 3V3 and 1V1 pours on In2 (the 1V1 pour is just under the MCU), and another ground pour on the back. All routing is on the front and back. The pours improve signal return paths and lower impedance.

Revision

I wasn’t fully sure the board would work with the voltage regulator wiring, so I redid it, changing the capacitor sizes so a copper trace fits between them, the way the datasheet wants.

07Testing

When the boards arrived, I soldered the remaining parts. With a multimeter, the 5 V pin read about 5 V and the 3.3 V pin read about 3.3 V, confirming the LDO works. The 1.1 V reading across the capacitor next to the inductor was also correct.

I then had Claude Opus 5.0 write example code to test the SPI display, the four buttons, and the onboard LED, and everything worked. I also tested every GPIO in code, and Arduino IDE recognized them all. Two things to fix: the display’s female headers weren’t soldered well, so the display has to be pushed down to work, and the display’s orientation was reversed in the design.
