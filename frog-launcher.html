01Highlights

  • Led software for a four-person embedded arcade game on a Raspberry Pi Pico, then took on CAD correction and laser cutting of every structural part.
  • Rewired and debugged the whole button and LCD circuit after half the inputs failed at prototype review, and traced an apparent Pico failure to excess LED current.

03The game and my role

Our game is a spin-off of Frog Launcher. The player gets three frogs and stands a set distance from the machine. There are three lily pads to land on and two alligators to avoid. The lily pads are worth 1, 2, or 3 points depending on how hard they are to land on, and the alligators are worth −1. Score four points with three launches and you win; otherwise you lose and get no coins. Roofs and trees in the way make it harder. My role was coder, but it expanded as the project went on.

Outcomes

  • Experience with laser cutting and AutoCAD
  • Pico troubleshooting, coding, and design and power rules
  • Basic construction skills
  • Working with others and communicating issues

04What I built

I was directly responsible for the LCD that shows the score, but I ended up responsible for the whole circuit. By the end of milestone 2, the wires were a mess and half the buttons weren’t working. I thought it would be an easy fix, but I couldn’t troubleshoot the tangle, so I rewired the whole board, which took a long time.

I learned to use the laser cutters at EXP and laser cut all the pieces and boards. The AutoCAD file was in the wrong units at first, which made the circles far too big, so I fixed the file. I added the reset button during milestone 4 and wrote all of the code for the project myself, which was challenging but fun.

I also helped with general construction, electronics, and painting to make it look better. Arabella and I refined the project during milestone 4, adding the roof, the tree obstacle, and more polish.

05Pitfalls and what I’d do differently

The score kept counting. In the prototype, pressing a button kept adding to the score. I asked Claude for help and used a for loop to stop it.

Redoing the circuitry. This was the biggest one. The wires were too short and loosely placed, so half the buttons didn’t work when I went to fix the code. I couldn’t tell which wires were connected because tape covered the extensions, and there was no wire management or photos to help. It took about three hours to work out the original circuit, rewire it, and fix the errors. I also thought I had burnt the Pico, but the big LED was drawing too much power, so we switched back to the small one. Next time: manage the wires properly and document how the circuit is built.

Too easy. People were throwing the frog at the end of the board and using it as a backboard to hit the hard lily pad. On Professor Davis’s advice, we added a roof and a few obstacles for milestone 4.

Laser cutting. On my first trip to FYELIC, the holes came out far too big because the AutoCAD units were inches instead of centimeters (you can see the mistake in the appendix). I had also cut the board for a big LED, then had to switch back to a small LED for power reasons.

06What went well

We finished on time for milestone 3, when a lot of projects weren’t done. Players of all kinds had fun, loved the cute frogs, and enjoyed the look of the game. Competitive players especially liked it: after milestone 4 it looked easier than it was, so people wanted to go five times in a row before they finally won.

Marketing

I’d market it to kids around 6 to 14. Basic hand-eye coordination is the only real requirement, and anyone who enjoys arcade games would like it, so it would fit places like Chuck E. Cheese and Dave & Buster’s. I’d focus the marketing on the cute frogs, and the game works in any region.

Thanks

A big thank you to Arabella for all her help, and thanks to Nathaly and Alex for contributing their parts. This wouldn’t have been possible alone.
